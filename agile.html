Project overview

Our research in semester 1 showed that Golden Arrow (GABS) commuters lose time queuing at ticket booths to load trips onto their Gold Cards. GoldenWay lets commuters buy and load trips online, while five staff roles support them the way a real GABS operation would.

Team
Group 26, five developers, each owning one app role
My work
Commuter Home and Support screens (August), then the Agent role: the live support chat and the ticket queue where agents claim, resolve and escalate commuter queries
Stack
React, Tailwind CSS, JavaScript, Supabase (PostgreSQL with row-level security), deployed on Vercel
Method
One-week sprints, Kanban board on Trello, stand-ups on class days, weekly retrospectives
Customer
Our lecturer supervisor, who gave feedback at every demo
Links
Live app · Team Trello board · Team charter (PDF) · My logbook (PDF)

1. Agile Team Charter

The charter is our team's social contract: why we work together, what we value, how we run our sprints and where our boundaries are. We wrote version 1.0 in May for the business idea and replaced it with version 2.0 on 20 October to match how we actually worked during development.

Vision: "To empower working-class commuters and students in Cape Town with a seamless, cashless, and convenient digital transit payment experience, so that no one misses their bus."

Product goal for this phase: a deployed, database-backed app where every role has working features, demonstrated to our lecturer supervisor before the October portfolio submission.

Core values (based on the CPUT graduate attributes)

  • Ubuntu: if someone is stuck, we help them before moving on.
  • Respect: in code reviews we comment on the code, not the person.
  • Transparency: blockers come up at stand-up, and Trello shows the real state of the work.
  • Accountability: each member owns their role from design to a working screen.
  • Ethical capability: simulated payments, no full card numbers stored, test data only, row-level security.
  • Resilience: a retrospective every week, acting on at least one lesson.

Working agreements

  • Stand-ups on class days using the three logbook questions.
  • One-week sprints: planning on Tuesday, retrospective on the last class day.
  • Each member works on their own branch; nobody pushes to main.
  • Pull requests reviewed by the Team Lead, merged one at a time, build checked after each merge.
  • Shared files have one owner; ask before changing them.
  • Every table has row-level security; migrations go live before testing.
  • Can't finish a task? Tell the team on WhatsApp before the deadline, not after.

Definition of Done: a card is done when the feature works against the real database with no hardcoded data, lint and tests pass, the pull request is reviewed and merged, the screen is checked on a mobile layout, and the owner has updated Trello and their logbook.

Reflection (10%)

The part of the charter that affected my work most was the rule about branches and shared files. In week 5 a merge pushed the whole frontend folder to GitHub instead of its contents. I had to clone the repository again, move onto my own branch and push my screens from there. After that I was much more careful about what I committed. In week 6 the team agreed that every shared file has one owner. When my Support chat ran off the bottom of the page, I traced it to the shared dashboard layout, so I raised it with the team instead of changing a file that wasn't mine. In week 7 I did the same with the auth context and the shared API file when connecting my chat to the database.

Where I fell short was the logbook agreement. The Definition of Done says a card isn't finished until the owner has written about it in their logbook. I didn't keep that up in the busy weeks at the end of the project, so my ticket queue work isn't recorded properly even though I built it. In a job I'd want the same kind of written agreement. It meant nobody had to guess who owned what, and when something broke, the rules told us who to talk to instead of who to blame.

Reflection (10%)

My first real task breakdown was the Home and Support screens in week 4. I split each Figma mockup into sections (header, rides card, current pass, recent activity) and built them one at a time. That made a big screen manageable. But I'd only broken the work down by what it looked like, not by how it had to fit into the project. I built both screens in plain CSS with their own bottom navigation, then found the project used Tailwind and a shared dashboard layout. Both screens had to be reworked.

When I was given the agent live chat in week 6, the user story kept me focused on what mattered: an agent answering a commuter quickly. The task that took longest was connecting the chat to real data. I first had to understand how the rest of the team called Supabase, so my code would follow the same pattern. The ticket queue story, "so every query has an owner", made it clear the chat on its own wasn't enough: a ticket had to belong to an agent and move between states. Next time I'd add "check how the existing code does it" as the first task on every card, before any building starts.

View the product backlog on Trello

Reflection (10%)

The backlog I started with in August was the commuter one, and my Support screen was really a mockup: a chat thread and a call view with nothing behind them. When the team made GoldenWay multirole on 11 August, that screen gained a new purpose, because there was now an Agent on the other side of the chat. Seeing the Agent stories next to each other with their priorities told me what order to work in. Live chat was the Must have, so it came first. The ticket queue was a Should have and followed once the chat worked.

The priorities mattered most after the campus closure. We came back on 15 September with two days to go before the integration demo, and parts of the app, my chat included, were still simulated. Because the backlog was ordered, I didn't have to decide what to work on. I spent those days learning the team's database structure and connecting the chat, because that was the highest-priority thing on my side that wasn't real yet. I'd use a prioritised backlog the same way at work: as a way to stop guessing when time runs out.

Reflection (10%)

Sprint 5 taught me the most about planning. On the Tuesday I planned to build the Support screen. I did build it, but most of the sprint went on things I hadn't planned: converting both screens from plain CSS to Tailwind, removing the duplicate navigation, and recovering from the merge that pushed the whole frontend folder. The work was still done by Friday, but only because I gave up the polish I'd meant to do. My sprint plan had only counted building new things, not fitting them into everyone else's code.

Sprint 6 showed a different problem: dependency. My chat ran off the page because of the shared layout, and I could only fully test my screens once other people's work had been merged. Then the three-week closure split the project in half, and sprint 7 had to start with catching up on how the project had changed. If I planned these sprints again, I'd keep a day free in each one for merging and fixing. I'd also bring up dependencies at planning instead of finding them mid-week.

Reflection (10%)

My logbook is most detailed for weeks 4 to 7, and those are the weeks I can best explain now. It shows the same blocker coming back in different forms: in week 4 I wasn't sure my screens would fit the project, in week 5 they didn't (wrong styling, a second nav bar), in week 6 I was waiting on shared files, and in week 7 I was learning a structure that had changed while campus was closed. Read together, the entries show I kept building before checking how my work connected to everyone else's. I only noticed that pattern by reading the log back.

The logbook also helped me restart after the closure. On 15 September my first entry was about reviewing what the team had built, and it listed exactly what was still missing on my side. That told me where to begin. My weakness is that I stopped logging when the project got busiest. Weeks 8 to 10, when I built the ticket queue, have no entries, and that's the work I most needed a record of. I treated the logbook as admin rather than part of the work. Next time I'd write a few lines at the end of each class day, because two minutes on the day is easier than trying to remember a month later.

Bringing it together

Looking across the charter, user stories, backlogs and logbook, the main thing I take away is that on a team, how my work connects to everyone else's matters as much as the work itself. The Home and Support screens I built in August looked right but didn't fit the project, and my chat couldn't be finished until shared layout and backend work landed. Each artefact helped with a different part of that. The charter told me who owned what, the stories and backlog told me what to build first, and the sprint board showed what everyone else was doing.

In my first job I'll start every task by looking at how the existing code is done. I'll raise dependencies early, keep to my own branch, and ask before touching a shared file. I'll also keep a short daily record of my work. When something goes wrong or I need to explain what I've done, that record is the evidence, and this project showed me what it's like not to have it for the weeks that mattered most.
